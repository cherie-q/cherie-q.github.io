Someone asked me how I write such clear documents.

My first answer was that I work on them with AI, going back and forth until they read well. It's not one prompt and a finished document.

But I kept thinking about the question. What was I actually doing in those conversations? And could I give someone else a way to do it too?

The answer wasn't a prompt. It was a process I'd never written down.

Writing it down became a toolkit: an internal plugin that helps AI write documents the way I would. Building it taught me something that goes beyond documents.

Look at the work I already had

I gathered the documents I had written recently and asked AI to look for patterns. What did they have in common? Could we turn those patterns into writing rules or pieces I could use again?

I wasn't trying to invent rules for good writing from scratch. I had a pile of documents. I wanted to work out what I'd been doing, so the method wouldn't live only in my head or in old chats.

Make something someone else could use

Then I brainstormed with AI: how could I package this so someone else could make similar documents?

We split it into four parts: shape, theme, components and profile. The toolkit comes with a default for each, and anyone can change any of them. My components, for example, include a little drawing of me.

The hard part was deciding what the defaults should be, and what to leave for each person to choose.

Then we built it. I had written down more of what I wanted AI to do. That wasn't yet a reason to trust it. I needed to use it.

Use it before sharing it

Before sharing it, I used it to write four documents. I still asked questions and made corrections, but this time I kept track of them.

I didn't count carefully. The first document needed about 20 of them. After that, the number dropped.

Each one showed me something: an instruction to improve, or a decision I still needed to make myself.

Is this just me, or would it help others?

AI tends to be verbose and flowery. That led to a rule in the default profile, the one everyone starts with: "direct, concrete, sentence-case headings." I wanted every document to start that way, not leave each person to keep asking for it.

Another correction felt more personal. AI was adding opinions on my behalf. I want what I write to be grounded in data, so "no unsupported editorial comments" went into my own profile. AI could draft, but it didn't get to decide what I believed. And a saved rule didn't mean I could stop checking the claims.

Was I correcting this document, improving the defaults, or telling it something about me?

Some choices are still open. My profile says that when writing gets wordy, review it for conciseness or consider a diagram. That might belong in the defaults too. Where a rule starts doesn't have to be where it stays.

What changed after I shared it

Once I was happy with it, I published the plugin and shared it.

I still use it to write documents at work, and it still doesn't get things right first time. But more of the back-and-forth is now about what I'm trying to say and how to explain it, not the style or layout.

It remembers my preferences, so I correct the style less often. Checking the claims and the story is still my job.

Beyond documents

In Copilot PR Squad, I wrote about building the process, not just finishing each piece of work. Building the toolkit showed me the same idea in everyday work. Documents are just one example.

Building it helped me see my own process clearly: what I could hand to AI, what still needed my judgement, and what the next attempt could teach me.
